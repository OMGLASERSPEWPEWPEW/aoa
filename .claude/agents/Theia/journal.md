## 2026-08-16 -- Color as Architecture: Sub-Brand Color Families Within a Single Product

### The Question

Art of Art now has two distinct content domains -- Shows and Classes -- each with its own visual language on the map and in the UI. Shows live in the existing amber/navy palette. Classes have claimed amber as their accent color (diamond markers, dashboard header, shimmer states, progress arcs, pipeline dots). This raises a structural brand question: how do you give distinct product areas their own color identities without fracturing the parent brand?

### What I Found

**The FedEx Model (Endorsed Brand Architecture).** FedEx is the canonical example. "Fed" stays purple across every division; "Ex" shifts color -- orange for Express, green for Ground, red for Freight, blue for Office. The parent identity (purple) is the constant; the variable color is wayfinding. Customers know they are still in FedEx, but they instantly know which FedEx. Notably, FedEx eventually consolidated back to just purple and orange, acknowledging that too many division colors diluted recognition. The lesson: color differentiation works, but the palette must stay small enough that each color remains meaningful.

**Material Design's Section Theming.** Google's Material Design 2 documentation explicitly supports using distinct primary colors for different areas within a single app. Their example: yellow for onboarding and content selection, blue for account-related screens, pink for courses. The structural insight is that color becomes spatial -- it tells you where you are, not just what you are looking at. This is color as wayfinding, not decoration.

**Duolingo's Gamified Color Coding.** Duolingo uses a 16-color palette with saturated secondaries (Yellow, Blue, Red, Purple) tied to different language skills and achievement states. Greyed-out icons bloom into full color as skills are completed. The key takeaway: color can encode both category (what kind of thing) and state (how far along). Their signature Duo Green anchors everything; the secondaries are satellites.

**Nike's Sub-Brand Ecosystems.** Jordan, ACG, SNKRS -- each has a distinct visual language (ACG's earthy tones with high-vis hits, Jordan's premium black-and-red gravity, SNKRS' event-driven neon energy). The Swoosh is the connective tissue. They prove that sub-brands can diverge dramatically in palette and tone as long as the parent mark and structural DNA remain consistent.

### What This Means for Art of Art

We are building what brand architecture calls an "endorsed brand" model within a single product. The parent brand -- deep navy shell, amber primary accent, warm Chicagoan voice -- is the constant. Content domains (Shows, Classes, and eventually Venues, Community) can each carry a secondary accent color as wayfinding. But the structural rules must hold:

1. **Navy is the container.** Every domain lives inside the same dark shell. This is our "Fed" -- the unchanging frame.
2. **Amber remains the primary accent.** CTAs, belt highlights, the Mentor avatar -- these stay amber everywhere. Amber is the brand color, not a domain color. The fact that Classes currently use amber as their domain color is fine for now (it is the first and only secondary domain), but if we add a third domain, Classes will need their own secondary.
3. **Domain colors are wayfinding, not identity.** A show card and a class card should both feel like Art of Art. The color difference tells you "you are in the Classes section," not "you have left the app."
4. **Three is the ceiling.** FedEx's lesson: more than three or four domain colors and you get noise, not signal. If we end up with Shows (current amber), Classes (amber for now, likely needs its own hue later -- teal? coral?), Venues, and Community, we need a tight palette of no more than three secondary accents plus the primary amber.

### Commitment

I will draft a "Domain Color Map" addendum to the brand identity configuration that codifies which hues belong to which content domains, with rules for when a domain earns its own color versus sharing the primary accent. This prevents ad-hoc color creep as the product grows. The map should be in place before a third content domain ships.
