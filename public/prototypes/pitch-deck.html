The problem

Theater feels like a locked room

Chicago has 250+ theater companies. Storefronts charge $10-35. Many let you usher for free. The scene is the most accessible in the country and almost nobody under 30 knows it.

There's no Letterboxd for plays. No Goodreads for scripts. No map that tells you "14 curtains are up within three miles of you right now, and three of them are free."

People who want to go track shows in Google Sheets. Their friends text them recommendations that get buried. A play they heard about in acting class has no way to find them when it's finally staged nearby.

The thesis: theater is inaccessible not because of price or geography, but because there's no curation layer, no social layer, and no visible path in for people without a theater school or friend group already inside the scene.

What already exists

The competitive landscape is wide open

We looked at every theater app on the market. None do what AoA does.

Spotlighter

Theater diary app

Basic show logging. Star ratings. No map, no social, no play-level tracking, no discovery. Essentially a spreadsheet with a nicer UI.

MISSING: map, feelings, social, plays, access info

Theatregoer

Logging app (UK-focused)

West End / Broadway focused. Log shows, leave reviews. No emotional vocabulary, no map, no community, no Chicago coverage.

MISSING: map, feelings, local scene, progression

BroadwayWorld "My Shows"

Feature on a news site (launched 2025)

The biggest theater site added a "My Shows" tab. It's a list. No map, no emotion system, no play-level tracking, no social.

MISSING: everything beyond a list

Letterboxd

Film diary + social (analog)

The gold standard for film tracking. Star ratings, lists, social. But it's for movies, which are one-to-one (one film = one thing to watch). Theater is one-to-many (one play = many productions).

ANALOG: great model, wrong medium. A play needs a Goodreads-like work/edition split, not a Letterboxd-like single-entity model

Goodreads

Book tracking + social (analog)

Tracks the work separate from the edition. "Want to Read" shelf notifies you. This is the right model for plays — but nobody's built it for theater.

ANALOG: right data model (work vs. edition), right shelf concept, wrong medium

Google Maps / Yelp

General maps

You can find a theater on Google Maps. You can't see what's playing tonight, whether it's free, or whether your friends went.

MISSING: "what's on" layer, relationship, access info

Nobody combines: a live map of what's playing tonight + Goodreads-style play tracking + an emotion-based rating system that doesn't reduce someone's year of work to 3.2 stars + a social layer of people you trust + access information that actually gets a broke 23-year-old through the door.

What AoA actually is

Three layers, one app

Layer 1

The Map

A live map of every theater in Chicago. What's playing tonight, what it costs, who's been, whether you can usher for free. The social layer over maps that theater doesn't have.

Layer 2

Goodreads for Plays

Track the play, not just the production. Add A Doll's House to "want to see." When a Chicago theater announces it, we tell you. Nobody else does this.

Layer 3

The On-Ramp

Progression that rewards going, not spending. Usher shifts count double. Free nights are first-class. No paywall between a newcomer and the scene.

Feelings, not stars

A five-star average tells you a show is a 3.8 and tells you nothing about whether you should go.

"31% unsettled, 24% held, 18% gutted" tells you exactly what kind of night it is.

It also lets a person be honest without being cruel. Bored is a color, not a one-star review of somebody's year of work. That matters in a small scene where the actors read everything.

◉ 12 feelings, fixed, not user-extensible ◉ Pick 1-3, order preserved everywhere ◉ Optional room volume: murmur/applause/standing ◉ Step 2 (writing) is always skippable ◉ Share-of-picks aggregation = percentages sum to 100

Getting closer to the work

Seven ranks. All free. Ushering counts double — it's the cheapest door into the scene and it puts a newcomer inside a building with the company.

The seating chart is a literal visualization: your lit seat moves toward the stage as you advance. The next rank is always phrased as an invitation, never a requirement.

THE SEVEN RANKS
STANDING ROOM
Sign up, finish onboarding
BALCONY
Log 1 show with feelings
MEZZANINE
3 shows across 2+ venues

6 shows, 3+ venues, 3 reflections
FRONT ROW
12 shows in one season, 2+ room kinds, 1 opening or 1 usher shift
GREEN ROOM
5+ reviews, ushered twice, 2 friends who logged
COMPANY
25+ shows over 2+ seasons, 8+ venues, sustained contribution

Non-negotiable: No criterion costs money. No leaderboards. No comparison. No streaks. No decay. Ranks never go down. Social criteria don't start until rank 5.

The work, above any one staging

"Want to see this play" — the Goodreads "Want to Read" button, but for plays. Our AI scraper monitors Chicago theater schedules weekly. When a venue announces this play, every user who wants it gets notified.

The aggregate spectrum shows how audiences felt across all productions. The timeline shows every Chicago staging we've tracked, with "YOU SAW THIS" for productions in your record.

This is the data model that makes AoA different from every competitor. Letterboxd treats each film as one entity. Goodreads separates work from edition. Theater needs the Goodreads model — the same play gets staged a hundred different ways.

Under the hood

How the AI scraper works

A weekly automated job visits 100+ Chicago theater websites, sends the HTML to an AI model, and extracts structured event data: title, dates, prices, cast, playwright. It then fuzzy-matches each event against our plays database.

The vision

Make theater feel as enterable as a movie

Not by dumbing it down. By building the map, the social layer, and the on-ramp that movies already have and theater doesn't.

A map that shows you what's on. A record that remembers how you felt. A scraper that finds the play your teacher told you about. A community that doesn't care how many shows you've seen — just that you went.
