# Conversation One: White Belt Guide to Chicago Theater

## The Problem — Getting In Without a Warm Intro

How do you access the art scene — specifically theater — when you don't know anyone? The intimidation factor is real, but it's mostly an illusion. Theater is the most accessible art form if you know where to show up. Unlike gallery openings or music industry events, theater literally sells tickets to the public.

In Chicago especially, theater people are *desperate* for audiences. Unlike New York or LA, Chicago theater isn't a stepping stone industry town — people do theater here because they *love* it. When you show up, you're not an outsider crashing a party. You're the reason the party exists.

---

## Entry Points, Ranked by Ease

1. **Just buy tickets.** Start going. See 2-3 shows a month. You'll develop taste fast.

2. **Talk to people after shows.** In Chicago storefront theater, the actors are often in the lobby after the show. Say "that was great" — you've just made a connection. This is not New York. People will talk to you.

3. **Volunteer to usher.** Many theaters let you usher in exchange for seeing the show free. You meet staff, you see everything, you spend nothing. This is the cheat code.

4. **Take classes (you're already doing this).** Your classmates ARE your scene. Some of them will be in shows. Go see their shows. Now you know people.

5. **Go to opening nights.** Usually the same ticket price but the energy is different — the company is there, there's often a reception after. Nobody checks credentials at the door.

## What to See: The Starter Curriculum

### The Big Institutions

- **Steppenwolf Theatre** — The legendary one. Founded by Gary Sinise, John Malkovich, Laurie Metcalf, Joan Allen. Known for raw, muscular, ensemble-driven work. This is the Chicago style that changed American theater. Go see whatever they're running. You'll understand what "Chicago acting" means.

- **Goodman Theatre** — The big downtown house. More polished, more diverse programming. World premieres and classics. Good place to see craft at scale.

- **Chicago Shakespeare Theater** (Navy Pier) — Shakespeare done well. Great for understanding the classics as an actor.

- **Court Theatre** (Hyde Park) — Academic but excellent. Deep dives into important plays. If you want to understand *why* a play matters, Court will teach you.

### The Storefront Scene (Where the Real Chicago Magic Lives)

Chicago is the only city in America with a thriving storefront theater ecosystem — small companies with 50-99 seat houses doing ambitious work on tiny budgets. This is where you'll fall in love.

- **A Red Orchid Theatre** — Intense, dark, visceral. Michael Shannon came out of here. If you like things that make you uncomfortable in the best way.

- **Steep Theatre** — One of the best small companies in the city. Consistently excellent.

- **Remy Bumppo Theatre Company** — Smart, literate, language-focused work.

- **TimeLine Theatre** — Only does plays set in or about the past, connected to present issues. Incredibly specific mission, consistently great.

- **Lookingglass Theatre** — Physical, visual, inventive. Adapts novels and creates original work. Ensemble member David Schwimmer co-founded it.

- **Writers Theatre** (Glencoe, just north) — Beautiful new space, incredible intimate productions.

### The Adventurous / Experimental

- **The Neo-Futurists** — Their show *The Infinite Wrench* (30 plays in 60 minutes) runs every weekend. Audience interactive, weird, fun, cheap. **Go here first.** The least intimidating entry point in Chicago theater.

- **The House Theatre of Chicago** — Big, imaginative storytelling. Makes you feel like a kid again.

- **Theater Oobleck** — Anarchist theater collective. No directors. Wild.

## Plays You Should Know (The Acting Student's Canon)

### The Canon — See These Performed, Don't Just Read Them

- **August Wilson** — His Pittsburgh Cycle is the most important dramatic achievement in American theater. Start with *Fences* and *Joe Turner's Come and Gone*. Chicago does Wilson beautifully.
- **A Streetcar Named Desire** (Tennessee Williams) — The play that invented modern American acting.
- **Death of a Salesman** (Arthur Miller) — You need to see this live at least once.
- **Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf?** (Edward Albee) — Four actors, one room, total devastation. Steppenwolf does this kind of play like nobody else.
- **'night, Mother** (Marsha Norman) — Two actors, real time, gut-punch.
- **A Raisin in the Sun** (Lorraine Hansberry) — Written in Chicago, about Chicago, essential.

### Contemporary Writers to Watch For

- **Tracy Letts** — Chicago's own. *August: Osage County* is the modern classic. Also an actor at Steppenwolf.
- **Annie Baker** — Her plays (*The Flick*, *John*) feel like real life happening in front of you.
- **Jeremy O. Harris** — *Slave Play* broke theater open.
- **Branden Jacobs-Jenkins** — *An Octoroon*, *Appropriate* — funny, smart, uncomfortable.
- **Sarah Ruhl** — Chicago-connected, poetic, surprising.
- **Tarell Alvin McCraney** — Wrote *Moonlight* (the film). His plays are lyrical and powerful.

## Life Stages of a Theater Person

### The 20s — The Hustle & The Magic

**Socially:** The golden era. You're in classes, showcases, seeing shows 4 nights a week because your friends are in them. Working a restaurant/bar job that lets you audition during the day. The community is tight — you date within it (everyone does, it's messy, it's fine). Opening night parties go until 3 AM. You do a play for $100 stipend and you don't care because you're *in it.*

**Personally:** You're discovering what kind of artist you are. You say yes to everything — Shakespeare, musicals, devised pieces, storefront dramas, sketch shows — figuring out your lane. Improv vs. theater vs. film is the big fork in the road (in Chicago, improv pays more reliably). There's a beautiful naivety — you really believe you can do this forever.

**The hard part:** Money is tight. You're comparing yourself to everyone. Your non-theater friends don't fully get it. You audition constantly and hear "no" way more than "yes." The emotional highs and lows are extreme.

### The 30s — The Reckoning & The Craft

**Socially:** The herd thins. Some people went to LA, New York, or left entirely. Your friend group gets smaller but deeper. You know *everyone* in the Chicago scene — it's a small town within a big city. You might be on the board of a theater company or helping run one. The parties are smaller but better.

**Personally:** Craft deepens. You stop trying to be impressive and start trying to be truthful. You take on bigger roles. You might get your Equity card (the actors' union) which opens some doors and closes others (you can't do non-union storefront without a waiver). The 30s question: **am I going to commit to this?** Chicago rewards that decision. Unlike NY/LA, you can actually afford to live here and do theater.

**The hard part:** You see peers booking TV/film. Money conversation gets real — health insurance, retirement, maybe kids. You start teaching to supplement income. The gap between "successful theater career" and "making a living" becomes impossible to ignore.

### The 40s — The Mastery & The Legacy

**Socially:** You're an elder now, even if you don't feel like it. 20-somethings look at you the way you used to look at people. You mentor whether you mean to or not. Your social life is more intentional. You might be running a company, directing, teaching at a university. The community respects you because you stayed. In Chicago, *staying* is the credential.

**Personally:** If you're still doing it at 40, you're probably good at it. The roles change — you're playing the parents now, not the lovers. This is actually liberating. The best work often happens here because you have nothing to prove. You understand the business. You know how to read a room, a script, a director.

**The hard part:** Your body changes. Eight shows a week hits different. The industry hasn't solved the money problem and now you have real expenses. Some people pivot to directing, writing, producing, teaching. The question shifts from "am I good enough?" to "is this sustainable?"
